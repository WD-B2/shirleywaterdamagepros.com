Mold Clearance Testing in Shirley, NY: What to Ask For

By the Shirley Water Damage Pros team · Published November 9, 2026

A buyer or lender may want proof that mold work was done and checked. Here is what a clearance is, who may perform it, and how to get a document that holds up.

☎ (934) 223-1875

A buyer's agent, an appraiser or a lender asks for a clearance, and a mold problem you thought was finished is back on the table. In Shirley, NY the paperwork is simple once you know who is allowed to write it. This guide explains what a clearance is, who does it, and what to request before anyone signs.

It is written for owners who have had mold work done or are about to, and who need a document that stands up in a sale or a loan. It promises no outcome, because the result depends on what the assessor finds.

A clearance, in plain terms

A clearance is the post-remediation assessment. A licensed mold assessor returns after the work is finished and checks whether the remediation plan was carried out and whether the area is ready to be used again. It is a judgment against the plan, made by someone who was not paid to do the cleanup.

What it includes depends on the project and on the assessor's plan. It may involve a visual inspection, moisture readings and sampling, and the assessor decides which. The result is a written report, and that report is what a buyer, lender or insurer may want to see.

A clearance is not a lifetime certificate. It describes the condition of the area on the day of the assessment. If moisture returns, so can the problem, which is why the water source has to be fixed first.

Why the grader cannot be the one being graded

Picture a home seller who also writes the inspection report. Even an honest company would be marking its own work, and a buyer or lender has little reason to trust that. New York writes the separation into its rules, covered in the local section below.

The structure works like bookends. An assessor sets the plan and the target before work starts. A contractor does the work. The assessor comes back and checks the result against the plan. Ask any company how it keeps those roles separate on your project, and get the answer in writing.

The usual order of events

Exact steps vary with the project, but most go in this sequence.

  1. Assessment and plan

    A licensed assessor inspects the area, looks for the moisture source, and writes the remediation plan with its scope and limits.

  2. Fix the water

    Work on mold goes nowhere while moisture keeps arriving. The leak, drainage or humidity problem is dealt with first.

  3. Remediation

    A separately engaged licensed contractor carries out the plan. This is the work described on the page for mold remediation, and you can ask any company whether its crews work to ANSI/IICRC S520.

  4. Post-remediation assessment

    The assessor returns, checks the area against the plan, and decides whether it is ready to be put back in use.

  5. Written report

    Keep the dated report with your closing or loan file, along with the plan and any moisture readings.

New York's license rule, and what to ask in Shirley

The New York State Department of Labor mold program requires mold assessors, mold remediation contractors and mold abatement workers to be licensed. The same individual may not perform both the assessment and the remediation on the same project. The licensed assessor writes the remediation plan and does the post-remediation assessment. Ask any mold company for its New York license and who does the assessment, and get both answers in writing.

Homeowners working on their own residence are exempt, and a mold project means more than ten square feet. If you cleaned a small area yourself, a buyer or lender may still ask for something on paper. Ask them early whether they want an assessor's report, and hire a licensed assessor if they do.

Lining the paperwork up with a sale or loan

Tell your agent, attorney or lender about the mold work early. Each may want something different, such as the plan, the final report or the assessor's license, and requirements vary from deal to deal. Ask what they need before you hire anyone, so the plan can be written with that in mind.

Do not book the clearance for the day before closing. An assessment that finds more work to do takes time to resolve, and nobody can promise the result in advance. Keep the drying records as well. Moisture readings from the drying phase show the water was dealt with and are easy to attach to the file.

Closing up the walls after the report

Remediation often removes drywall and trim, so a room may sit opened up when the assessment happens. Ask the assessor whether the plan calls for rebuilding before or after the post-remediation assessment, because it depends on the project. When it is time to close the walls, wall and drywall water damage repair covers the rebuild, and the moisture readings should be normal before anything is closed in.
