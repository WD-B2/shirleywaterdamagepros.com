Water Damage Restoration in Smith Point, Shirley, NY

Smith Point is the bay-side residential peninsula at the south end of Shirley. This page covers what water damage looks like here and what to do first.

☎ (934) 223-1875

Smith Point is the residential peninsula at the south end of Shirley, running down William Floyd Parkway to the Smith Point Bridge, with mail addressed to Shirley, NY 11967. Residents have their own civic body, the Smith Point Peninsula Homeowners Association, and the name is also used for the nearby county park, which is a separate public site.

This page is about homes on the peninsula. It explains the kinds of water damage that show up near a bay shoreline, which parts of that picture are documented and which are not, and what to do in the first hours. For the wider area, see our main Mastic Beach and service area pages.

What the county documents about the Smith Point shoreline

Suffolk County Planning studied the Mastic and Shirley peninsula in its Narrow Bay Floodplain Protection and Hazard Mitigation Plan, published in 1997.

It puts the 100-year flood plus wave effects at about 8.8 feet above the NGVD datum at Smith Point, compared with 16.2 feet at Moriches Inlet, and notes a V zone along the bay with an A zone landward of it. The plan estimates that the 100-year floodplain covers about a quarter of the 6,750-acre study area.

That study predates Superstorm Sandy and flood maps have changed since, so treat it as background, not as a statement about your street. Neighborhood-specific FEMA flood-zone status is not verified here. You can check your own address on the FEMA flood map and the Suffolk County GIS layers, and the county also publishes a hurricane surge map, revised in 2022, that covers the Shirley shoreline.

During Sandy coverage, a regional environmental advocate described the Mastic and Shirley peninsula as one of the lowest-lying communities on Long Island. That is a regional description, not a count of flooded Smith Point homes, and no home-specific flood report for the peninsula was found in the research behind this page.

Where water damage can come from in a bay-side home

Bay-side living does not mean every loss is a flood. Most calls start with something inside the house, and the first move depends on the source.

First steps before help arrives

These steps assume it is safe to move around. If water is deep, near outlets or the panel, or may be sewage, stay out and call.

Stop the source

Close the main water valve if a pipe or appliance is leaking and you can reach it safely.

Keep clear of hazards

Do not stand in water near outlets, cords or the panel, and do not use electrical equipment in wet areas.

Photograph the damage

Take wide and close photos of the source and the wet areas before anything is moved.

Protect what is dry

Lift small valuables, papers and electronics off wet floors when it is safe to do so.

Call the line

Phone (934) 223-1875 and describe the water. For gas smells or electrical danger, call 911 or the utility first.

Extraction and drying after a loss

Water extraction removes standing and pooled water first, using pumps and extractors. Drying follows, with air movers and dehumidifiers placed according to moisture readings. Our structural drying page explains how readings guide the work and when materials are removed instead of dried.

Contaminated water, including flood water and sewage, changes the job. Household fans and an ordinary vacuum are not enough for it, and porous materials that were soaked by it are often removed. The crew assesses the hazards before any restoration begins.

Townhouses, associations and shared walls

Parts of the peninsula are subdivisions and townhouse complexes with property owners clubs or homeowner associations, such as Woodland and Smith Point Estates. If you live in one, check your association documents for who handles exterior walls, roofs and common areas, and tell the association promptly about a leak that may involve a neighbor.

Shared walls and ceilings can carry water between units, so photograph any damage that crosses a wall and keep the association and your insurer informed in writing.

Documenting a loss for your insurer

Photos, moisture readings and a written scope can help describe what happened and what work was done. Whether a loss is covered depends on the cause and your policy. A typical homeowners policy does not cover flood, so ask your insurer what applies and read the terms before you need them.
